## Background

In February 2026, the Supreme Court held that certain tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) were unlawful. The Court of International Trade then required a refund process covering all importers, not just those that sued. About 330,000 importers paid an estimated $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries.

Refunds are already showing up in company results: [Levi's recorded about $80 million](/articles/2026-10-14-tariff-refunds-levis-80m-how-companies-reinvest/) in one quarter.

## How CAPE works

CBP built CAPE (Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries) into the ACE portal and launched Phase 1 on April 20, 2026.

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Prepare | Confirm entries were filed in ACE with a liquidation status, and banking details are on file in ACE |
| 2. Build the list | Use CBP's CSV template to list entry numbers; up to 9,999 per declaration, with no supporting documents needed |
| 3. Submit | The importer of record or its filing broker uploads a CAPE Declaration in ACE |
| 4. File-level checks | ACE confirms the submitter, the entry list and formatting; a failure rejects the whole file |
| 5. Entry-level checks | ACE confirms each entry exists, declares a dutiable IEEPA Chapter 99 code and isn't duplicated; a failure rejects only that entry |
| 6. Refund | Approved refunds are combined into one ACH payment with statutory interest |

## Which entries qualify in Phase 1

**Included:** certain unliquidated entries, and certain entries liquidated within 80 days of the CAPE submission date.

**Excluded from Phase 1** (some may be handled in later phases or by other remedies):

- Entries flagged for reconciliation, and reconciliation summaries
- Entries on a drawback claim
- Entries under an open protest
- Entries not filed in ACE or without an ACE liquidation status
- Certain antidumping and countervailing duty entries pending liquidation
- Entries with final liquidation

Timing matters: entries age out of the 80-day window as they liquidate, so filing early protects more of them.

## Where procurement comes in

Most procurement teams don't file customs entries, but they can affect how much of the refund their company keeps.

**If your company is the importer of record:**

1. Ask trade compliance or your customs broker for a list of entries with IEEPA duties and their liquidation status.
2. Confirm CAPE declarations have been filed for every eligible entry, and track rejections.
3. Agree with finance how refunds are booked and whether any go back to customers under your sales contracts.

**If your suppliers were the importers of record:**

1. List suppliers that added tariff surcharges or price increases tied to IEEPA duties in 2025 and early 2026.
2. Ask whether they filed CAPE claims and what they expect to recover.
3. Review contracts for clauses that require duty savings or refunds to be passed back.
4. Negotiate credits, especially where surcharges were itemized separately.

## What IEEPA refunds don't change

The IEEPA tariffs were replaced by other measures, including a temporary Section 122 surcharge that expired July 24, 2026, the [Section 301 forced-labor tariffs](/articles/2026-10-12-section-301-forced-labor-tariffs-what-buyers-pay/) on 60 economies, and expanded [Section 232 tariffs](/guides/section-232-tariffs-explained-steel-aluminum-copper-pharma-semiconductors-wood/). A refund for past duties doesn't mean current landed costs are falling.
