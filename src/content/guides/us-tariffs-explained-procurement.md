## How U.S. tariffs work

A tariff is a tax on imported goods, paid to U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) by the **importer of record** when goods enter the country. It is usually a percentage of the goods' customs value (an *ad valorem* duty).

Every import starts with a **Most-Favored-Nation (MFN)** rate set by its classification in the Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States (HTSUS). On top of that base rate, the government can add special duties under trade laws. In 2026, two laws do most of that work: **Section 232** and **Section 301**.

## Section 232: product-based tariffs

Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 lets the president restrict imports that threaten national security. It is applied **by product**, not by country, although some partners negotiate lower rates.

As of October 2026, Section 232 duties cover steel, aluminum, copper, autos and parts, wood products, semiconductors, medium- and heavy-duty vehicles and patented pharmaceuticals.

Two changes in 2026 matter most to buyers:

- **Metals on full value.** Since April 6, 2026, steel, aluminum and copper duties apply to the full customs value of derivative products, not just their metal content. Rates are tiered at 50%, 25% and 15% depending on the product annex. Products with 15% or less metal by weight are excluded.
- **Pharmaceuticals.** A default 100% duty applies to patented drugs and their active ingredients, with lower rates for the EU, Japan, South Korea, Switzerland and the UK, and for companies with U.S. onshoring or pricing agreements. Generics are exempt for now. See our [pharma tariff coverage](/articles/2026-10-12-section-232-pharma-tariff-now-applies-all-importers/).

## Section 301: country-based tariffs

Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 lets the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) respond to another country's unfair trade practices. It is applied **by country**.

- **China tariffs (since 2018).** Duties of typically 25% on goods on Lists 1–3 remain in force. A shrinking set of **product exclusions** has protected some goods; the last 178 [expire Nov. 10, 2026](/articles/2026-10-12-section-301-china-exclusions-expire-nov-10/) unless USTR extends them.
- **Forced-labor tariffs (since July 24, 2026).** An additional 10% or 12.5% on imports from 60 economies, depending on whether a country has a forced-labor import ban. Section 232 goods, USMCA-qualifying goods and inputs used in U.S. manufacturing are among the exemptions. See [what buyers are paying](/articles/2026-10-12-section-301-forced-labor-tariffs-what-buyers-pay/).

## What happened to IEEPA and Section 122

The International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) was the basis for the broad "reciprocal" tariffs of 2025. The Supreme Court ruled on Feb. 20, 2026 that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs, and those duties ended. Refunds of the roughly $166 billion collected are being processed through CBP and the Court of International Trade.

The administration then used **Section 122** of the Trade Act of 1974, which allows a temporary surcharge of up to 15% for 150 days. A 10% surcharge ran from Feb. 24 to July 24, 2026, and expired when Congress did not extend it.

## How tariffs stack: a worked example

Duties are generally additive. Consider a $100,000 shipment of a Chinese-origin component with a 3% MFN rate, on Section 301 List 3, with no exclusion after Nov. 10:

| Layer | Rate | Duty |
|---|---|---|
| MFN (base) | 3% | $3,000 |
| Section 301 China | 25% | $25,000 |
| Section 301 forced labor | 12.5% | $12,500 |
| **Total** | **40.5%** | **$40,500** |

If the same part were sourced from Mexico and qualified under USMCA, the Section 301 layers would not apply. If it were a steel derivative, the Section 232 rate would apply and the forced-labor layer would not. This illustration is simplified; confirm your actual stack with a licensed customs broker.

## What procurement teams should do

1. **Get classification right.** The HTS code decides every layer. Audit your top 50 imported SKUs and get binding rulings where classification is unclear.
2. **Map origin, not just supplier.** Country of origin is where goods are substantially transformed, not where they ship from. Request origin documentation from suppliers.
3. **Claim every exemption.** USMCA qualification and the U.S.-manufacturing-input exemption are worth real money. Make sure your broker is claiming them.
4. **Write tariff clauses into contracts.** Decide who absorbs new duties, how quickly price adjusts, and what happens if a duty is refunded.
5. **Model landed cost, not unit price.** Compare suppliers on total landed cost including duty, freight and inventory carrying cost. Our [Cost Pressure Monitor](/cost-monitor/) tracks the underlying price trends.
6. **Track the calendar.** Effective dates and expirations move landed cost overnight. Treat them like contract renewal dates.
