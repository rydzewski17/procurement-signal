## Why it matters

If you buy any of the 178 Chinese-origin products still excluded from Section 301 tariffs, your landed cost could jump by roughly 25 points on shipments entered from Nov. 10, with no grace period for goods already on the water.

## The details

The U.S. Trade Representative last extended the remaining exclusions on [Nov. 26, 2025](https://ustr.gov/about/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2025/november/ustr-extends-exclusions-china-section-301-tariffs-related-forced-technology-transfer-investigation), pushing their expiration from Nov. 29, 2025 to Nov. 10, 2026. That extension followed the U.S.–China trade arrangement announced on Nov. 1, 2025.

The exclusions run through 11:59 p.m. ET on Nov. 9. Entries filed after that pay the underlying Section 301 duty, which is typically 25% for goods on Lists 1, 2 and 3, according to a [September analysis by Coppersmith Brockelman](https://www.coppersmith.com/section-301-exclusions-november-2026-deadline-update/).

What's different this year is the silence. Before the 2025 extension, USTR published a Federal Register notice inviting public comment weeks ahead of the deadline. As of mid-September 2026, no comparable notice had appeared, which trade counsel are reading as a warning sign. An extension is still possible, but the window for a normal comment process is closing.

Important limits on what an exclusion does:

- It removes only the Section 301 layer. MFN duties, Section 232 tariffs and any antidumping/countervailing duties still apply.
- It applies at the specific product description in the exclusion annex, not to a whole HTS heading. Many importers assume coverage they don't have.
- Electronics buyers are among the most exposed. Bare printed circuit boards under HTSUS 8534.00.00 are on the list, and [Altium estimated](https://resources.altium.com/p/tariff-reset-pcb-workarounds) their duty could climb from 10% to a combined 35% if the exclusions lapse.

## What buyers should do

- **Confirm exposure now.** Ask your customs broker or trade team to match your purchases against the exclusion annex at the 10-digit level, including the product description.
- **Re-run landed cost without the exclusion** for every affected SKU so pricing, budgets and should-cost models reflect the worst case.
- **Check contract terms.** Look for tariff pass-through, price adjustment or Incoterms language that decides who absorbs the increase.
- **Weigh pull-forward buys carefully.** Goods must be *entered* by Nov. 9, not shipped. Transit times from China already make late-October bookings tight, and extra inventory carries its own cost.
- **Watch the Federal Register.** A USTR notice is the only reliable signal of an extension. If none appears by late October, plan for the duty.

## The bigger picture

The exclusions are one of the few remaining pressure valves in the China tariff structure. Since the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs in February, the administration has leaned on statutes with firmer footing, including Section 232 on metals and pharmaceuticals and a [new Section 301 forced-labor action](/articles/2026-10-12-section-301-forced-labor-tariffs-what-buyers-pay/) covering 60 economies. Letting the China exclusions expire would fit that pattern of narrowing exceptions rather than adding new ones.
