## Why it matters

If you buy any of the 178 Chinese-origin products still excluded from Section 301 tariffs, your landed cost could jump by 7.5 to 25 points on shipments entered from Nov. 10, with no grace period for goods already on the water. The exclusions are narrow, so the bigger risk is often confusion: importers claiming coverage they don't have, or missing coverage they do.

## The details

**The deadline.** The U.S. Trade Representative last extended the remaining exclusions on [Nov. 26, 2025](https://ustr.gov/about/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2025/november/ustr-extends-exclusions-china-section-301-tariffs-related-forced-technology-transfer-investigation), with the notice published in the Federal Register on Dec. 1. That pushed expiration from Nov. 29, 2025 to 11:59 p.m. ET on Nov. 9, 2026. The extension followed the U.S.–China trade arrangement announced on Nov. 1, 2025.

Entries filed on or after Nov. 10 pay the underlying Section 301 duty: typically 25% for goods on Lists 1, 2 and 3, and 7.5% for List 4A, on top of normal duties. "No grace period," Coppersmith Brockelman wrote in a September analysis.

**The silence.** USTR usually opens a public comment window 60 to 90 days before deciding on an extension; the last cycle had a comment notice in September 2025. As of mid-October 2026, we have found no comparable notice, which trade counsel read as a warning sign. USTR has also described the exclusions as a temporary bridge to help importers move sourcing away from China, not a permanent feature. An extension is still possible, but the window for a normal process is nearly closed.

## What is still covered

The 178 exclusions fall under two codes, according to Great Lakes Customs Law:

| Code | Exclusions | What they cover |
|---|---|---|
| HTSUS 9903.88.69 | 164 product-specific | Narrow industrial machinery and components (for example certain compressors, DC blowers and electric motors with set power or size limits), a subset of medical products carried over from COVID-era grants, specialty chemicals, some foods such as certain frozen seafood, and vehicle climate-control parts |
| HTSUS 9903.88.70 | 14 solar equipment | Capital equipment used to make solar cells and modules, such as wafer slicing, deposition and testing equipment, but not finished solar cells |

**What is not covered:** EVs and EV batteries, consumer electronics and most List 4A finished goods, semiconductors and chipmaking equipment beyond solar, steel and aluminum, and apparel and footwear. Electronics teams should still check carefully: bare printed circuit boards under HTSUS 8534.00.00 are on the list, and [Altium estimated](https://resources.altium.com/p/tariff-reset-pcb-workarounds) their duty could climb from 10% to a combined 35% if the exclusions lapse.

**Limits on what an exclusion does:**

- It removes only the Section 301 layer. MFN duties, Section 232 tariffs and any antidumping or countervailing duties still apply.
- It applies to the exact product description in the annex, at the 10-digit HTS level, including technical limits such as dimensions, power, materials and end use. A partial match is not covered.
- The exclusion code replaces the underlying List code on the entry line. Reporting both can trigger a rejection or a later duty bill.

## What procurement teams should do

- **Confirm exposure now.** Ask your customs broker or trade team to match your purchases against the exclusion annex at the 10-digit level, including every technical parameter in the description.
- **Re-run landed cost without the exclusion** for every affected SKU so pricing, budgets and should-cost models reflect the worst case.
- **Check contract terms.** Look for tariff pass-through, price adjustment or Incoterms language that decides who absorbs the increase.
- **Weigh pull-forward buys carefully.** Goods must be *entered* by Nov. 9, not shipped. Transit times from China already make late-October bookings tight, and extra inventory carries its own cost.
- **Look back as well as forward.** If you paid Section 301 duty on goods that qualified, unliquidated entries can be corrected and recently liquidated entries may be protested within 180 days.
- **Watch the Federal Register.** A USTR notice is the only reliable signal of an extension. If none appears by early November, plan for the duty.

## Quick landed-cost check

For a product with a $100,000 customs value and a 3% MFN duty, the duty bill rises from $3,000 to $28,000 if a List 1–3 exclusion lapses, and to $10,500 for a List 4A product. That is our illustrative calculation; your rates depend on classification and any other duties that apply.

## What to watch next

- **A Federal Register notice.** Any extension would most likely appear as a USTR notice before Nov. 10. Its absence by early November is a strong signal the duty returns.
- **The U.S.–China trade truce.** The broader arrangement expires Jan. 10, 2027, which could reshape China duties beyond the exclusions.
- **Entry timing.** Watch in-transit shipments that could clear before or after Nov. 10 and coordinate with your broker on filing dates.

## The bigger picture

The exclusions are one of the few remaining pressure valves in the China tariff structure. Since the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs in February, the administration has leaned on statutes with firmer footing, including Section 232 on metals and pharmaceuticals and a [new Section 301 forced-labor action](/articles/2026-10-12-section-301-forced-labor-tariffs-what-buyers-pay/) covering 60 economies. Letting the China exclusions expire would fit that pattern of narrowing exceptions rather than adding new ones.
