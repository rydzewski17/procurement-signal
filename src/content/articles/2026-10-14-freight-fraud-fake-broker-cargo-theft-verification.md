## Why it matters

The biggest cargo theft risk is no longer a stolen trailer in a parking lot. It's a convincing phone call. Fraud-based theft exploits gaps between shippers, brokers and carriers, so procurement's carrier and broker vetting is now a frontline control.

## The details

On Oct. 3, a truck driver hauling about 29,000 pounds of frozen seafood worth more than $273,000 received calls and texts from someone impersonating the legitimate freight broker. The sender told the driver to divert part of the load to North Arlington, New Jersey, for a supposed transfer. Police responding to a report of suspicious activity found pallets being moved from the refrigerated trailer into a 26-foot box truck. The entire shipment was recovered, and two people were arrested. Investigators found the person directing the diversion had no connection to the brokerage. How the scammers obtained shipment details is still under investigation.

The case landed days before the third annual Freight Fraud Awareness Day on Oct. 14, supported by Verified Carrier, Truckstop, Northland Insurance, Travelers and others. Industry security experts quoted by FreightWaves describe a shift from physical theft to deception: stolen identities, double brokering, deceptive pickups and cyber-enabled schemes that let criminals identify valuable loads before pickup. Gary Thomas of SPG Cargo & Logistics said digital tools let criminals manipulate identities from almost anywhere: "We went from a traditional bad guy waiting at the truck stop." The FBI's Memphis Cargo Theft Task Force has described cyber-enabled schemes that identify valuable shipments before pickup. Retired Los Angeles County sheriff's detective Gerardo Pachuca said some offenders learn how legitimate operations work before exploiting their loopholes.

Chuck Forsaith of the Pharmaceutical Cargo Security Coalition said pharmaceutical losses were once "almost exclusively what we would call straight theft" but now rely more on fraud, and that stolen drugs can re-enter legitimate commerce without required quality controls. Recent cases reported by FreightWaves include a cloned $60,000 shipment that redirected 41,000 pounds of glycerin in Illinois.

**Sharing without exposing defenses.** Andrey Drotenko of Verified Carrier said the awareness day is a moment "to slow down, compare notes, and just act together," and that companies can discuss incidents without revealing the safeguards criminals might exploit, according to FreightWaves. Other cases FreightWaves has linked to the trend include a man sentenced to two years for a $3.5 million Amazon fraud using 23 trucking accounts, and $1 million of stolen BNSF and Union Pacific rail cargo found at a Los Angeles-area shoe retailer. GenLogs, FreightValidate and Freight Bites also support the campaign, which runs stopfreightfraud.com.

## Where fraud gets in

| Stage | Common scheme | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Carrier onboarding | Stolen or rented operating authority, look-alike companies | Identity verification beyond the authority number; check recent ownership and contact changes |
| Load booking | Double brokering to an unknown carrier | Contract terms banning re-brokering; tracking that ties the truck to the booked carrier |
| Before pickup | Spoofed emails or calls changing pickup details | Confirm changes through a known contact, never the incoming message |
| In transit | Calls to the driver ordering a "transfer" or diversion | Drivers told that diversions require written confirmation from a named contact |
| Delivery and payment | Fake invoices or changed bank details | Verify bank changes by phone with a known contact; dual approval |

## A verification checklist for the shipping dock

| Moment | Check |
|---|---|
| Booking | Carrier name, authority and insurance match the broker's confirmation |
| Arrival at pickup | Driver ID, truck and trailer numbers match what the carrier gave you |
| Before loading | The carrier's dispatcher confirms the driver by phone, using a number on file |
| Any change after pickup | Confirmed in writing by a named contact at the booked broker, called back on a known number |
| Delivery | Seal numbers and piece counts match the bill of lading |

This checklist is our suggestion. Share it with shipping teams and your brokers so everyone applies the same rules.

## What to put in broker contracts

- **No re-brokering** without written consent, with the hauling carrier disclosed on every load.
- **Carrier vetting standards** the broker must apply, including identity checks beyond authority numbers.
- **Liability and insurance** that clearly covers theft by fraud, not only physical theft.
- **Incident notice** within a set number of hours and cooperation with law enforcement.
- **Audit rights** over the broker's carrier records for your loads.

## Check your insurance

Cargo policies and carrier liability terms don't always cover losses where a shipper or broker was tricked into releasing freight. Ask your risk team and insurer how a fraudulent pickup or diversion would be treated, and whether your broker's contingent cargo coverage applies.

## What procurement teams should do

- **Verify after acceptance.** Require that any change to pickup, delivery address or routing is confirmed through a known contact at the booked broker or carrier, never through the number in a new message.
- **Ban unauthorized re-brokering.** Put double-brokering prohibitions and disclosure of the hauling carrier in broker contracts, with audit rights.
- **Vet continuously.** Check carrier authority, insurance and identity at onboarding and again periodically. Fraudsters use real carrier identities.
- **Tier high-value loads.** For pharmaceuticals, electronics, food and other targeted freight, add tracking, seals and approved-carrier lists.
- **Share incidents.** Report attempts to carriers, brokers and insurers so patterns spread quickly.

## What to watch next

- **How the scammers got shipment details.** The investigation could point to compromised load boards, email or broker systems.
- **Carrier verification tools.** Expect more brokers and shippers to require identity verification at pickup, not just at booking.
- **Pharmaceutical cargo.** Watch for tighter chain-of-custody requirements for high-value and regulated loads.

## The bigger picture

Freight fraud grows when markets tighten and shippers rush to cover loads with unfamiliar capacity, which describes today's [truckload market](/articles/2026-10-14-truckload-contract-rates-52-week-high-bid-season/). Procurement can reduce exposure by consolidating with vetted providers and building verification into every handoff, not just the first booking.
