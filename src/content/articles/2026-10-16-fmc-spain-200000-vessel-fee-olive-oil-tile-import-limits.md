## Why it matters

A U.S. regulator is proposing to limit imports of everyday Spanish goods over a shipping dispute. Nothing changes yet, but olive oil, ceramic tile, natural stone, fragrance and confectionery from Spain now carry policy risk that should be priced into 2027 sourcing plans.

## The details

**The finding.** The [Federal Maritime Commission](https://www.fmc.gov/articles/fmc-proposes-actions-to-address-unfavorable-shipping-conditions-in-spain/) said on Oct. 14 that its investigation found "conclusive evidence" that Spain denied U.S.-flagged vessels access to its ports, creating conditions unfavorable to shipping in U.S. foreign trade. The vessels involved sail under MARAD's Maritime Security Program. The FMC opened the investigation in December 2024.

According to Baird Maritime, the *Maersk Denver* and *Maersk Seletar* were refused entry in November 2024, and the *Maersk Hartford* skipped a planned Spanish call later that month. Maersk Line vessels in the program averaged 7.25 Spanish port calls a month before November 2024; since July 2025, their calls at Algeciras have almost stopped.

**Spain's position.** In its submission, Spain argued that Spanish, EU and international law require it to stop the transit of defense and dual-use goods, that it applies this regardless of flag and targets containers rather than ships, and that diplomacy through the State Department is the right forum. The FMC rejected those arguments, saying compliance with foreign law is not a defense under the statute.

**Remedy one: a vessel fee.** The [Federal Register notice](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/10/15/2026-21067/investigation-into-conditions-affecting-shipping-in-the-foreign-trade-and-denial-of-entry-of-vessels), published Oct. 15, proposes a $200,000 fee each time a Spanish-flagged vessel enters a U.S. port from a foreign port. If the fee goes unpaid, the FMC could ask the Department of Homeland Security to refuse clearance to that vessel or other Spanish vessels. Baird Maritime notes the amount approximates the inflation-adjusted value of a $100,000 fee from a 1997 case against Japanese port practices; the statutory ceiling is $2,364,503 per voyage.

**Remedy two: import limits.** The FMC proposes limiting U.S. imports of Spanish goods in five tariff chapters. The notice does not specify a quota or ban; it says the Commission could adopt broader, narrower or targeted limits, and could ask DHS to deny entry to or detain vessels carrying covered goods. The draft also allows the fee to rise, or categories to be added, if Spain retaliates.

## The goods at risk

| HTS chapter | Product group | 2025 U.S. imports from Spain | Notable lines |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | Oils and fats | $1.15 billion | Olive oil (15.09): $836.9 million |
| 33 | Perfumery and cosmetics | nearly $940 million | Perfumes and toilet waters (33.03): nearly $700 million |
| 69 | Ceramics | $634.7 million | Ceramic tiles (69.07): $523.7 million |
| 68 | Articles of stone | $270.6 million | Heading 68.10: $176.9 million |
| 17 | Sugar and confectionery | $138.8 million | |

Import values are from the Federal Register notice. Together the five chapters total about $3.1 billion, our calculation. The FMC excluded retail medicines (subheading 3004.90) and static converters and transformers (8504.40), citing U.S. health needs and a transformer shortage.

## Exposure check for category managers

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Do we buy any of the five chapters from Spain, directly or through distributors? | Distributor and private-label supply often hides country of origin |
| What share of that category comes from Spain? | Olive oil and porcelain tile are concentrated categories |
| Are alternative origins qualified? | Food specs and building specifications often name the product or origin, so switching takes time |
| Do our contracts say who bears the cost of import restrictions? | Change-in-law and force majeure wording decides it |
| Does any of our freight move on Spanish-flagged ships? | A $200,000 per-call fee would be passed through somewhere |

## What procurement teams should do

- **Pull 12 months of spend by origin** for HTS chapters 15, 17, 33, 68 and 69, including goods bought through distributors and co-packers.
- **Ask suppliers to confirm origin** and whether any product ships from or through Spain, including blends packed elsewhere.
- **Qualify a second origin** for high-exposure items, starting with food service olive oil and specification-driven tile and stone for construction projects.
- **Check contract terms.** Look for change-in-law and import-restriction language in 2027 renewals with Spanish suppliers and their U.S. importers.
- **Consider commenting.** Companies with material exposure can file by Dec. 14 on Regulations.gov, docket FMC-2026-0463.

## What to watch next

- **Dec. 14: comment deadline.** The final rule's scope, broad or targeted, will follow the record.
- **A hearing request from Spain.** The FMC has offered Spain a hearing; a negotiated settlement through diplomatic channels remains possible.
- **EU response.** The draft lets the FMC widen the measures if Spain retaliates, so watch for any EU-level reaction.

## The bigger picture

This is a shipping-law tool, not a tariff, but for procurement teams the effect is similar: a policy decision outside the commercial relationship that can change landed cost or availability with little notice. It adds Spain to a growing list of trade risks this year, from [Section 301 forced-labor tariffs](/articles/2026-10-12-section-301-forced-labor-tariffs-what-buyers-pay/) to the [China exclusions expiring Nov. 10](/articles/2026-10-12-section-301-china-exclusions-expire-nov-10/).

For food and construction categories that rely on Mediterranean supply, the lesson is the same as with [EU deforestation rules](/articles/2026-10-07-eudr-deforestation-regulation-december-30-2026-deadline-suppliers/): know your origins at the line-item level before a regulator asks.
