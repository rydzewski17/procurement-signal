## Why it matters

Furniture, cabinetry and fixtures show up in facilities, construction, hospitality and retail fit-out budgets. A scheduled jump from 25% to 50% on imported kitchen cabinets and vanities, and to 30% on upholstered wooden furniture, would raise landed cost sharply for projects delivering in 2027. Orders placed now for delivery next year will land under the higher rates.

## The details

Section 232 tariffs on wood products took effect Oct. 14, 2025, under Proclamation 10976. They set:

| Product | Rate through Dec. 31, 2026 | Scheduled from Jan. 1, 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| Softwood timber and lumber | about 10% | No change announced |
| Upholstered wooden furniture | 25% | 30% |
| Kitchen cabinets and vanities | 25% | 50% |

The increases were originally due on Jan. 1, 2026. A proclamation issued Dec. 31, 2025 delayed them by one year, citing "productive negotiations of agreements with multiple countries," according to a Troutman Pepper Locke analysis. It left room for different treatment for countries that reach agreements addressing the national security concern. The delay did not reduce current rates.

**How the rates were set.** The underlying action, Proclamation 10976, was signed Sept. 29, 2025 after a Section 232 investigation into timber, lumber and wood products, according to KPMG. It imposed 10% on certain softwood timber and lumber, 25% on certain upholstered wooden products and 25% on kitchen cabinets and vanities, with the higher rates originally scheduled for Jan. 1, 2026. The White House posted the delay on Dec. 31, 2025, and the 25% rates have stayed in place since.

**Why the delay may or may not repeat.** The administration tied the postponement to "productive negotiations of agreements with multiple countries." That suggests any further relief is more likely to come through country agreements, with different rates by origin, than through a blanket delay. Importers should track which supplier countries reach agreements before year-end.

**What's covered.** The tariffs apply to specific product descriptions in the proclamation's annex, not to all wood products. Confirm classifications for each cabinet, vanity and upholstered item with your customs broker; finished furniture that isn't upholstered, for example, may be treated differently.

## What it means for landed cost

For a cabinet order with a $200,000 customs value, the duty would rise from $50,000 at 25% to $100,000 at 50%, before freight and normal duties, by our calculation. For $200,000 of upholstered furniture, it would rise from $50,000 to $60,000.

## Pull forward or wait? A break-even example

Entering goods before Jan. 1 saves the difference between the current and scheduled rates, but you pay to hold the stock. For the $200,000 cabinet order above, entering early saves $50,000 in duty. Against that:

| Months the stock sits before you need it | Holding cost at 1.5% a month (example) | Net saving from entering early |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | $9,000 | $41,000 |
| 6 | $18,000 | $32,000 |
| 12 | $36,000 | $14,000 |

The 1.5% monthly holding rate is an illustrative assumption covering storage, capital and handling; use your own figure. These are our calculations. For cabinets, pulling forward pays in most cases where you have the space and the cash. For upholstered furniture, where the saving is only five points, it rarely pays to hold stock for more than a few months.

## Imported versus domestic: where the break-even moves

At 25%, an imported cabinet with a $100 customs value costs $125 plus freight and any other duties. At 50%, the same cabinet costs $150. A U.S.-made cabinet quoted at up to about $150, delivered on comparable terms, is now competitive before freight differences, by our calculation. Many buyers who ruled out domestic cabinetry on price should re-quote it for 2027 projects, and check domestic lead times and capacity before committing.

**Selling into Europe?** EU deforestation rules take effect Dec. 30, 2026 and cover wood products, so companies that both import into the U.S. and export to the EU face two sets of documentation requirements at the same time. See our [EUDR coverage](/articles/2026-10-07-eudr-deforestation-regulation-december-30-2026-deadline-suppliers/).

## What procurement teams should do

- **Classify every wood product SKU.** Confirm which products fall under the cabinet, vanity or upholstered furniture provisions.
- **Re-price 2027 deliveries.** Use the scheduled rates for anything entering after Dec. 31.
- **Weigh pulling forward imports.** Entering goods before Jan. 1 locks in 25%, but adds inventory cost and storage.
- **Ask domestic suppliers for quotes.** U.S.-made cabinets avoid the tariff and may be competitive at a 50% rate.
- **Add tariff change clauses** to project contracts so increases are shared or passed through transparently.

## Questions to ask your furniture and cabinet suppliers

1. Which of the products you sell us are classified as kitchen cabinets, vanities or upholstered wooden furniture under the proclamation?
2. What is the country of origin for each, and could origin change if a country reaches an agreement with the U.S.?
3. How will your price change on Jan. 1, and will you show the duty separately from the product price?
4. Can you ship 2027 project orders before year-end, and at what storage cost?
5. Do you have a U.S.-made or alternative-origin option, and what are its lead times?

## What to watch next

- **Country agreements** announced before Jan. 1 that could change rates by origin.
- **Another delay**, which the administration has used once already.
