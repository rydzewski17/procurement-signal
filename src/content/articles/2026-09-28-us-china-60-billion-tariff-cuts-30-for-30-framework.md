## Why it matters

This is the first concrete tariff relief from the U.S.–China talks this year, and it targets consumer and household categories that many procurement teams still source from China. But the list is narrow, the start date isn't set, and the trade it covers has already fallen sharply. Treat it as a planning input, not a reason to reverse diversification.

## The details

China's Commerce Ministry announced the plan on Sept. 28, after President Xi's Sept. 25 visit to Washington. It was negotiated through the Board of Trade, a government-to-government mechanism set up after President Trump's May visit to Beijing. Each side will cut tariffs on about $30 billion of the other's imports, roughly $60 billion of two-way trade, against $415 billion of total two-way goods trade last year.

**What's on the lists:**

| Side | Size of list | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| U.S. cuts on Chinese goods | 77 product entries | Fireworks, household goods, toys, kitchenware, sporting equipment |
| China's cuts on U.S. goods | 1,619 items | Meat, seafood, dairy, wheat, corn, sorghum, coal, timber, medical equipment |

About 90% of covered products would get most-favored-nation treatment, according to China's Commerce Ministry. The two sides also extended their trade truce until January.

**The trade has already shrunk.** The $30 billion targets are based on 2024 trade. Covered Chinese goods fell from $32.3 billion in 2024 to $23.2 billion in 2025, and covered U.S. goods from $29.7 billion to $19.3 billion. Bloomberg Economics' Chang Shu called the macroeconomic scale "negligible." U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said the deal improves market access for "about 30 percent of US exports to China."

**Side commitments.** Soybeans are excluded, but China committed to buy 25 million metric tons of U.S. soybeans a year through 2028 and at least 10 million metric tons of U.S. coal in 2027 and again in 2028, according to the White House. A new agricultural working group is due to meet by the end of 2026. Export controls and Taiwan remain unresolved.

## What it means by category

| Category | Likely effect | Our read |
|---|---|---|
| Housewares, kitchenware, toys | Lower duties once in force | Re-quote China-origin SKUs on the list; keep alternate suppliers |
| Sporting goods | Lower duties on listed lines | Check exact tariff lines before changing sourcing |
| Fireworks and seasonal goods | Lower duties | Timing matters for 2027 seasonal buys |
| Everything not on the list | No change | Section 301, forced-labor and other duties still apply |

## What procurement teams should do

- **Map your China spend to the final list.** Match at the tariff-line level once the notice is published; a product family on the list may not mean every SKU is.
- **Model landed cost both ways.** Compare China-origin cost with the cut against your diversified sources, including freight and lead time.
- **Don't unwind diversification yet.** The cuts depend on legal steps not yet complete, and the truce runs only to January.
- **Watch the expiring exclusions.** Separately, [178 Section 301 exclusions expire Nov. 10](/articles/2026-10-12-section-301-china-exclusions-expire-nov-10/), which can raise costs on other China-origin goods.

## What to watch next

- **Effective dates.** Look for U.S. and Chinese notices completing domestic procedures.
- **The January truce deadline.** Two more Trump–Xi meetings are expected; a lapse could change the picture quickly.
- **Agricultural purchases.** Progress toward China's farm purchase goals will signal whether the framework holds.

## The bigger picture

The "30-for-30" list shows how U.S.–China trade policy now moves: small, negotiated lists rather than broad resets. For procurement, the lesson is to manage China exposure line by line, with origin, tariff and alternative supplier data ready to act on when lists change.
