## Why it matters

Distributors sit between thousands of suppliers and their customers. When one fails, suppliers face unpaid invoices and customers face supply gaps. Noble's case is a textbook example of a risk procurement teams often miss: a distributor that bought inventory ahead of a long contract, then lost the contract early.

## The details

Noble Supply & Logistics and 10 affiliates filed for Chapter 11 on Aug. 30 in U.S. Bankruptcy Court in Delaware. The Boston-based company serves more than 4,000 U.S. government customers across about 150 contracts, plus commercial and international customers, through a network of more than 13,000 suppliers. It supplies fasteners, hardware, maintenance, repair and operations (MRO) products and commercial off-the-shelf items, plus logistics services.

The petition estimates assets of $100 million to $500 million and liabilities of $500 million to $1 billion. Noble had no restructuring agreement, exit financing or stalking-horse bidder at filing and said a sale, reorganization or another transaction were all possible. It planned to keep operating on cash collateral and asked the court for authority to pay wages, critical vendors and certain lien claimants.

**What went wrong, according to Noble's court filing:**

- **A shortened contract.** In 2021 the Defense Logistics Agency awarded Noble a fastener and hardware supply contract for multiple weapons systems with an estimated potential value above $1 billion. Noble bought heavily to meet delivery requirements, sometimes waiting up to 18 months between buying inventory and getting paid. In December 2024, DLA said it would not exercise the next option when the term ended in June 2026, cutting an expected 10-year program to four years.
- **No inventory buyback.** Noble says DLA did not complete closeout procedures or an end-of-contract inventory buyback, leaving more than $70 million of inventory and purchase obligations from that contract and about $100 million overall.
- **A second termination.** On Aug. 27, DLA terminated about $400 million of orders under a separate contract. Noble intends to appeal.
- **Strained suppliers.** Liquidity problems forced Noble to delay vendor payments.

Noble cut about $30 million of operating expenses and raised $25 million from subordinated noteholders in June 2025, but that wasn't enough. Talks with Bain Special Situations didn't produce a deal.

## Warning signs to watch in your own distributors

| Signal | What it can mean | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Slower payments to their suppliers | Cash strain | Ask key manufacturers about distributor payment behavior |
| Concentration in one large customer or contract | Single point of failure | Ask for customer concentration in reviews |
| Large inventory build for a specific program | Cash tied up | Ask how inventory is funded and who bears obsolescence |
| Receivables factoring or new subordinated debt | Liquidity stress | Monitor credit reports and lien filings |
| Failed sale or investment talks | Limited options | Track news and court filings |

## What procurement teams should do

- **If Noble supplies you:** confirm open orders, delivery commitments and contact points with the restructuring team, and qualify alternate sources for critical parts.
- **If you supply Noble:** check whether you qualify as a critical vendor and track the claims process.
- **For all distributors:** add customer concentration and inventory funding to supplier risk reviews, not just credit scores.
- **Write buyback terms into long contracts.** If you ask a distributor to stock for a program, agree up front what happens to that inventory if the program ends early.

## What to watch next

- **Bids for Noble's business** and whether a buyer keeps its supplier network.
- **Noble's appeal** of the $400 million termination.
- **Defense procurement policy**, as the Pentagon pushes to bring more commercial suppliers into defense work.

## The bigger picture

Long-term contracts transfer risk to whoever builds inventory. When the buyer can shorten the contract and the supplier can't recover its inventory, the supplier carries all of it. Fair buyback and termination terms protect both sides, and the supply base that depends on them.
