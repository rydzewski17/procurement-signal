## Why it matters

Nearshoring to Mexico shortened supply chains on paper. In practice, it moved risk to the border. When qualified drivers and crossing capacity shrink, lead times for Mexican-sourced parts and finished goods stretch, and freight costs rise. Procurement teams that moved spend to Mexico need freight plans as deliberate as their supplier plans.

## The details

U.S. authorities have withdrawn visas from about 25,000 Mexican freight drivers, according to C.H. Robinson's market report, which cites the Mexican trucking association CANACAR. Patricio Gutierrez of TRAFFIX says the core problem is a shortage of qualified drivers holding B-1 visas and of trucks authorized to operate in the U.S. Many drivers are in their 50s and 60s, and security concerns and modest pay deter younger workers.

From Sept. 9 to 14, restrictions on southbound empty-truck crossings at Eagle Pass and Brownsville, Texas, reportedly cut daily commercial crossings from about 1,000 to 300–400 and left nearly 700 tractors stranded on the U.S. side. Some carriers rerouted through Laredo, adding miles, fuel and transit time.

Market indicators are tightening. In Laredo, FreightWaves SONAR's outbound tender volume index rose from about 40 in mid-September to about 52, and the rejection index turned positive. TRAFFIX's October cross-border index reads 54 for Mexico and 57 for Canada, where readings above 50 signal tight conditions. Some Canadian corridors rose 25% to 35% in about six weeks, and some carriers are handing back contracted freight because earlier rates no longer cover costs.

TRAFFIX expects tight capacity through the end of 2026 and into 2027.

## What procurement teams should do

- **Map border dependence.** List the parts and products that cross from Mexico, the crossings they use and the carriers that move them.
- **Lock committed capacity.** For critical flows, contract with carriers that hold U.S. authority and have qualified drivers, with volume commitments in both directions where possible.
- **Qualify more than one crossing.** Laredo, Eagle Pass and Brownsville behave differently under restrictions. Pre-approve alternates.
- **Add buffer for the fourth quarter.** C.H. Robinson warns driver availability could tighten further as fourth-quarter volumes rise.
- **Review contract rates.** If carriers are returning freight, rates below market won't hold. Renegotiate before service fails.

## What to watch next

- **Visa policy.** Further restrictions on B-1 visas for Mexican drivers would tighten capacity again.
- **Border crossing rules.** Another round of empty-truck restrictions at Eagle Pass or Brownsville would push more freight through Laredo.
- **Contract compliance.** Watch tender acceptance on cross-border lanes; TRAFFIX expects tight capacity into 2027.

## The bigger picture

Cross-border capacity is now a constraint on nearshoring strategy, alongside supplier capability and tariffs. Autonomous trucks are starting on the [Dallas–Laredo lane](/articles/2026-10-14-autonomous-trucks-laredo-lane-50000-hardware-cost/), but not at a scale that changes the 2027 picture. For now, the fix is contract structure and contingency planning.
