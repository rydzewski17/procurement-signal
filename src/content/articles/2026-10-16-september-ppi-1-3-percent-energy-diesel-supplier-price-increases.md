## Why it matters

September's producer price report is the clearest evidence yet that the energy shock is moving into what businesses pay each other. Price letters citing it will arrive over the next few weeks. The report also gives procurement teams the counterargument: outside energy, food and transport, input prices rose modestly.

## The details

**The headline.** The Producer Price Index for final demand rose 1.3% in September, seasonally adjusted, the [Bureau of Labor Statistics reported](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/ppi_10152026.htm) on Oct. 15. That is the largest monthly gain since a 1.7% rise in March 2022, according to Securities.io, and follows increases of 0.5% in August and 0.2% in July. Unadjusted, final demand prices were 6.3% higher than a year earlier.

**Goods carried it.** Final demand goods rose 2.6%, the largest increase since the series began in December 2009. Energy prices jumped 10.8% and accounted for more than 80% of the goods increase. Gasoline rose 20.3%, and BLS also listed higher prices for diesel, jet fuel, residential electric power and crude petroleum. Food rose 1.2%, although chicken eggs fell 39.7%.

**The rest of goods barely moved.** Final demand goods excluding food and energy rose 0.3%. Materials and components for manufacturing rose 0.4%, and materials and components for construction rose 0.5%. BLS listed thermoplastic resins among the items that fell.

**Earlier in the chain, it's steeper.** At the intermediate stage, which is what your suppliers buy:

- Processed goods rose 2.5% in the month and 14.1% over 12 months. Diesel fuel alone rose 21.0%, and processed fuels and lubricants 10.2%.
- Unprocessed goods rose 8.4% and are up 21.5% on the year, with crude petroleum up 28.3%.
- Intermediate services rose 0.9% and 6.3% on the year.

BLS's stage-based indexes show prices for the earliest production stages up 13.5% (stage 1) and 13.9% (stage 2) over 12 months, against 7.9% for stage 4, the closest to final demand. In our reading, that gap is cost that has not yet fully reached finished goods.

**Services and logistics.** Final demand services rose 0.8%. Transportation and warehousing services rose 3.4%, and BLS cited higher prices for truck transportation of freight. Trade margins rose 1.4%, including a 3.9% increase in margins for machinery and vehicle wholesaling, while margins for retailing fuels and lubricants fell 30.3% as pump prices rose faster than retailers could pass them on.

**Core.** Final demand excluding food, energy and trade services rose 0.5% in the month and 5.0% over 12 months.

The energy backdrop explains the timing. The [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/reports/oil-market-report-october-2026) says diesel hit records of about $220 a barrel in Europe and the U.S. in September and expects the market to stay tight well into 2027, as we reported in our [diesel outlook](/articles/2026-10-15-diesel-tight-into-2027-iea-houthi-aramco-strikes/).

## Where September's increases will show up

| Index (September change) | Who feels it first | Our read for category managers |
|---|---|---|
| Diesel fuel, intermediate (+21.0%) | Carriers, delivered-price suppliers, aggregates, chemicals | Fuel surcharges reset automatically; check tables, don't add fuel to base rates |
| Transportation and warehousing services (+3.4%) | Freight, 3PL, warehousing contracts | Expect accessorial and storage increases in 2027 renewals |
| Food, final demand (+1.2%) | Food service, ingredients | Mixed: eggs fell sharply while other items rose |
| Goods less food and energy (+0.3%) | Most direct materials suppliers | Weak case for broad increases; ask for evidence |
| Materials and components for manufacturing (+0.4%) | Components, fabricated parts | Modest; hold suppliers to the specific input that moved |
| Thermoplastic resins (lower) | Packaging, molded parts | Ask for decreases, not increases |

The interpretations are our assessment, built on the BLS figures.

## Questions to ask when a supplier cites the September PPI

Our [guide to price indexes](/guides/procurement-cost-inflation-price-indexes/) covers how to pick and write the index into contracts.

1. Which index do you use, and what share of our price does it represent?
2. Is the increase tied to energy and freight, or to your materials? Show the split.
3. If diesel falls back, how and when will the increase reverse?
4. Does the increase apply to the full price or only to the energy and freight component?
5. Which month's data are you using? BLS revises each month for four months after release.

## What procurement teams should do

- **Separate energy from everything else.** Accept fuel and freight pass-through through indexed surcharges, and push back on across-the-board increases that cite the headline 1.3%.
- **Ask for cost breakdowns.** Use the 0.3% rise in core goods and the 0.4% rise in manufacturing materials as the benchmark for non-energy cost.
- **Index, don't fix.** For energy-heavy categories, tie price adjustments to a named index with a reversal clause so increases come back out when energy eases.
- **Re-quote resin-based categories.** Plastic resin prices fell in September; packaging and molded-part suppliers have room to hold or cut prices.
- **Update budgets.** Use the 21.5% year-on-year rise in unprocessed goods and the stage 1–2 gap as a signal that more cost may reach finished goods in the next one to two quarters.

## What to watch next

- **ISM Manufacturing report for October, early November.** Its prices index, at 77.9 in September per our [ISM coverage](/articles/2026-10-01-ism-september-prices-index-77-9-supplier-lead-times/), shows whether suppliers expect to keep raising prices.
- **Nov. 13: October PPI.** It will show whether energy eased or kept climbing after the Houthi strikes on Saudi energy sites, and will revise the September figures.
- **Weekly DOE/EIA diesel prices.** The index most fuel surcharges follow; a sustained drop would reverse part of September's increase quickly.

## The bigger picture

For most of 2026, cost pressure was concentrated in metals under [Section 232 duties](/guides/section-232-tariffs-explained-steel-aluminum-copper-pharma-semiconductors-wood/) and in freight. September adds energy on top, and energy flows into almost every price procurement teams pay. The data also show where the pressure is not: core goods and manufacturing materials rose modestly, and resins fell.

That split is the negotiation.
