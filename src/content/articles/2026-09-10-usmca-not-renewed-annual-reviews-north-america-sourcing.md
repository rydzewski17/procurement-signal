## Why it matters

Nearshoring to Mexico rests on USMCA. The agreement hasn't ended, but it is no longer a settled 16-year framework. Annual reviews and bilateral talks mean rules of origin, metals measures and other terms could change before 2036, and procurement plans built on Mexican or Canadian supply should account for that.

## The details

**Update, Oct. 16: U.S.–Mexico tariff talks stall.** Progress toward a U.S.–Mexico deal that would lower U.S. tariffs on steel, aluminum and automobiles in exchange for Mexican concessions has stalled, the Wall Street Journal reported on Oct. 15, citing people familiar with the talks (as relayed by Reuters). Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has opposed significant cuts to the 25% tariff on Mexican autos and the 50% tariff on Mexican steel and aluminum, and objected to lowering duties on finished steel and aluminum products. Mexico's deputy trade minister, Luis Rosendo Gutiérrez, told the Journal the negotiations are making progress, and a Commerce spokesperson said the department is in "constant dialogue with Mexico on trade." For sourcing teams, our read is that relief on Mexican metals and vehicles should not be assumed in 2027 budgets.

The USMCA Free Trade Commission held the agreement's first six-year joint review on July 1, 2026. U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said the United States "did not agree to renew the USMCA in its current form." Mexico and Canada both supported a 16-year extension.

Because the U.S. did not confirm an extension, the parties now review the agreement every year through July 1, 2036, when it would expire. They can still agree to extend it at any point.

**Bilateral talks instead.** The U.S. chose separate negotiations with each partner. With Mexico, three rounds were completed by late July, with a fourth scheduled for September. Topics include:

- Automotive rules of origin and automobiles
- Steel, aluminum and derivative products
- Economic security
- Labor and environment
- Electronic payment services, regulatory compatibility and agriculture

As of late July, no formal U.S.–Canada talks had been held.

**Investment protections.** The investment chapter has no general survival clause after termination, according to IISD's analysis, so a future U.S. withdrawal could end protections for existing investments.

**How the extension clause works.** Article 34.7 gives the agreement a 16-year term from its 2020 entry into force and requires a joint review at the six-year mark, according to a White & Case analysis. If all three countries confirm in writing that they want another 16 years, the term resets without renegotiation. Because the U.S. didn't confirm, the agreement now gets a joint review every year until 2036, and each one is a fresh chance to extend. Current rights stay in force throughout: preferential tariffs, rules of origin, investment protections and dispute settlement.

**What the rounds have covered.** The first U.S.–Mexico round, in Mexico City on May 28–30, covered auto rules of origin, steel and aluminum and economic security. The second, in Washington on June 16–17, advanced rules of origin for certain industrial goods and began conceptual talks on agriculture, labor, environment, steel, aluminum and autos. A third round followed in Mexico City in late July.

**Canada's position.** Canada signaled support for a 16-year renewal in a June 1 letter from minister Dominic LeBlanc. Its priority is U.S. sectoral tariffs on steel, aluminum, autos and lumber, and it had not begun substantive text-based negotiations with the U.S. by late July.

**Why the investment chapter matters for sourcing.** Most bilateral investment treaties include a sunset period that keeps protecting existing investments for years after termination; USMCA's Chapter 14 does not, IISD notes. That matters for companies that have built or financed supplier plants in Mexico, and IISD links the investment stakes to critical minerals supply chains. Under Article 34.7.4, the extension can still be confirmed at any time before 2036.

## Three scenarios for North American sourcing

| Scenario | What changes | What it means for sourcing |
|---|---|---|
| Extension agreed at a future review | Term resets to 16 years | Nearshoring plans regain a long horizon |
| Rolling reviews with targeted changes | Tighter auto rules of origin, metals terms or labor provisions | Some parts lose preference unless suppliers change inputs |
| Breakdown of talks | Higher risk of withdrawal or loss of preference | Duty and investment risk on Mexican and Canadian supply |

These scenarios are our framework. The second is the most likely near-term path given the bilateral talks under way.

## What losing preference would cost

USMCA preference means qualifying goods enter duty-free. A part that no longer qualifies pays the normal U.S. duty for its classification, and for many goods that now includes Section 232 or other duties layered on top. For a sourcing team, the useful exercise is to list your top Mexican and Canadian parts by spend, note the duty each would pay without preference, and multiply. That figure is the value at risk in each annual review, and the number to bring to suppliers when discussing origin compliance and contract terms.

## What procurement teams should do

- **Audit USMCA claims.** Make sure certificates of origin are current and that suppliers can support them. Preference also shields qualifying goods from the [Section 301 forced-labor tariff](/articles/2026-10-12-section-301-forced-labor-tariffs-what-buyers-pay/).
- **Build change clauses into Mexican and Canadian supply agreements.** Spell out who absorbs cost if origin rules or duties change.
- **Stress-test nearshoring business cases.** Run scenarios where preference is lost on key parts.
- **Keep an eye on the border.** Separate from USMCA, [cross-border trucking capacity is tight](/articles/2026-10-14-mexico-cross-border-trucking-capacity-visa-restrictions/).

## Questions to ask Mexican and Canadian suppliers

1. Which of our parts do you certify as USMCA-originating, and under which rule of origin?
2. For auto parts, what is the regional value content today, and how much room is there if the threshold rises?
3. Which inputs come from outside North America, and could you switch them?
4. Who bears the duty if a part loses preference after a review?
5. How quickly can you produce origin documentation if U.S. Customs asks for it?

## What to watch next

- **Whether U.S.–Mexico talks restart on metals and autos.** A deal cutting the 50% steel and aluminum and 25% auto tariffs would change landed cost on Mexican supply; for now, Commerce opposition has stalled it.
- **The fourth U.S.–Mexico round** and any agreed changes to auto rules of origin.
- **U.S.–Canada talks**, which had not formally started by late July.
- **Jan. 12, 2027 comment deadline.** USTR opened public comments on the operation of the agreement ahead of the 2027 joint review (Federal Register document 2026-20341, docket USTR-2026-0595). Comments and requests to testify at a public hearing are due by 11:59 p.m. EST on Jan. 12, 2027; USTR will post the hearing date on its website. Companies with North American supply chains can put their rules-of-origin and cost data on the record.
- **The 2027 annual review**, the next chance to confirm an extension.

## The bigger picture

USMCA has moved from a long-term framework to a rolling negotiation. That doesn't make Mexico or Canada less attractive for sourcing, but it raises the value of flexibility: dual sourcing, documented origin and contracts that anticipate rule changes.
