## How the steel market works

Most steel that procurement teams buy reaches them in one of three ways: directly from a mill on a contract, through a steel service center that cuts and processes it, or embedded in a part from a fabricator or component supplier. Mills set base prices for products such as hot-rolled coil, cold-rolled sheet, plate and bar, and add extras for grade, size and processing. Service centers add their own margin and handling, and pass mill increases through quickly.

U.S. production is split between electric arc furnace "minimills," which melt scrap, and integrated mills, which make steel from iron ore. That mix matters for cost: scrap prices drive minimill costs, while iron ore, coke and energy drive integrated mills. It also matters for availability: some specialized products, such as the grain-oriented electrical steel used in transformers, come from very few plants.

Because steel is a commodity with published market prices, most large buyers price it with some form of index: a base price tied to a market benchmark plus fixed extras and processing charges. Flat annual price agreements are common in smaller contracts, but they tend to lag the market in both directions.

## What is driving prices now

**Trade measures.** Since April 6, 2026, imports of most steel articles carry a 50% Section 232 duty on their full customs value. That raises imported landed cost directly and gives domestic mills room to raise their prices toward the import price. Our [Section 232 guide](/guides/section-232-tariffs-explained-steel-aluminum-copper-pharma-semiconductors-wood/) explains how the duty applies to steel derivatives.

**Demand.** U.S. manufacturing has expanded for nine straight months, according to the ISM survey, and respondents in September named steel among commodities rising in price and several steel categories as short. A machinery maker told ISM that AI and data center demand is "stretching domestic steel capacity."

**Price momentum.** Producer prices for steel mill products were up 23.4% over the 12 months through August and hit a new high, with the three-month pace running above 40% annualized, according to our [Cost Pressure Monitor](/cost-monitor/steel/).

## Where the negotiating leverage sits

Suppliers hold it. When imports are expensive, demand is firm and some grades are short, mills and service centers can choose customers and push increases through. Buyers who ask for big discounts risk losing allocation. Leverage comes back to procurement through volume commitments, payment terms, forecast visibility and flexibility on grade and size, which make you an easier customer to supply.

## Supply risk: what could go wrong

- **Allocation on specific grades.** Short grades can go on allocation, with mills favoring contract customers.
- **Longer lead times.** ISM's September survey put capital equipment lead times at 176 days and production materials at 84 days, and steel availability was cited as a constraint on output.
- **Tariff changes.** Country agreements or new Section 232 actions can change landed cost quickly for imported steel and steel-intensive parts.
- **Single-source specialty steel.** Electrical steel, some alloys and certain plate sizes have very few domestic producers.

## How to buy it now

1. **Separate critical from commodity grades.** For the grades that would stop production if short, secure volume first: contract with a domestic mill or a service center with mill allocation, and give forecast visibility in exchange.
2. **Index the price.** Tie the steel content of the price to a published benchmark, with adjustments in both directions and a trigger band so small moves don't cause constant repricing.
3. **Make suppliers show the steel share.** For fabricated parts, only the steel content should move with the index; labor and overhead have different drivers.
4. **Check landed cost, not just quotes.** For imported parts, add the 50% duty on full value before comparing with domestic offers.
5. **Shorten review periods.** Quarterly reviews keep price closer to market than annual ones in a fast-moving market.

## What to watch

- Monthly producer price data, to see whether the 40%-plus pace slows.
- Section 232 changes, including country agreements and derivative product lists.
- Mill lead times and allocation notices, especially for grades named as short in the ISM survey.
