## How chip supply works

Semiconductors cover very different products. Logic chips and microcontrollers run devices; analog and power chips manage signals and energy; memory chips, DRAM and NAND flash, store data. Each has its own supplier base, pricing pattern and lead times. Memory behaves most like a commodity: a few large producers, prices that swing with supply and demand, and contract prices reset quarterly.

Most procurement teams buy chips indirectly, inside IT hardware, controls, vehicles and equipment, or through electronics manufacturing services providers and distributors. That makes exposure easy to miss until a supplier announces allocation or a price increase.

## What is driving prices now

**AI demand for memory.** Goldman Sachs expects U.S. data center capacity additions to rise from 6.4 gigawatts in 2024 to 36.3 GW in 2027, and that demand is shifting DRAM capacity toward AI servers. TrendForce expected conventional DRAM contract prices to rise 90% to 95% in the first quarter of 2026 and another 58% to 63% in the second.

**The broad index is falling.** At the same time, U.S. producer prices for semiconductors overall were down 3.1% on the year through August, according to our [Cost Pressure Monitor](/cost-monitor/semis/). The contrast shows how concentrated the shortage is.

**Tariffs.** Section 232 tariffs on semiconductors are in effect; check product-specific scope with your broker.

## Where the negotiating leverage sits

Suppliers have the edge on memory and memory-heavy products. On other chips, procurement has more room, especially where the index is falling. The practical answer is to split the category: protect memory supply, and re-quote the rest.

## Supply risk: what could go wrong

- **Allocation**, where suppliers ration memory to customers with long-term agreements.
- **Missed ship dates** on finished equipment held up by one missing chip.
- **Forced redesigns** when a part goes end-of-life or can't be sourced.
- **Price increases** on PCs and servers; PC makers warned of 15% to 20% increases from the second half of 2026.

## How to buy it now

1. **Find memory in your bill of materials** and in supplier products.
2. **Secure allocation in writing** for critical items, with quantities and dates.
3. **Extend forecasts** to suppliers so they can secure their own supply.
4. **Approve equivalent configurations** for memory size or brand.
5. **Pull forward IT refreshes** where budgets allow, and lock pricing for a set period.

## What to watch

- Quarterly memory contract price reports.
- New memory fabrication capacity, which takes years.
- The government response to industry groups' letter on memory supply.
