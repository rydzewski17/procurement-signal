## How the aluminum market works

Aluminum reaches procurement teams as sheet, plate, extrusions, castings or as part of finished components. Its price has two layers. The first is the metal itself, traded on global exchanges. The second is a regional premium that reflects the cost of delivering metal to a market, including freight and duties. In the U.S., that premium is commonly called the Midwest premium. Fabricators then add a conversion charge for rolling, extruding or casting.

That structure is useful for buyers: it lets you separate the part of the price that moves with the market (metal plus premium) from the part that reflects the supplier's own costs and margin (conversion). Contracts that bundle everything into one fixed price make it hard to tell whether an increase reflects metal or margin.

## What is driving prices now

**The duty.** Imports of most aluminum articles carry a 50% Section 232 duty on full customs value. Because the U.S. imports a large share of its aluminum, the duty feeds through to the domestic price via the regional premium, not just to imported parts.

**A pullback from the peak.** U.S. producer prices for aluminum mill shapes were 5.9% below their June high in August, though still 27.3% above a year earlier, according to our [Cost Pressure Monitor](/cost-monitor/aluminum/). The three-month pace was negative.

**Still rising for many manufacturers.** In ISM's September survey, respondents still listed aluminum among commodities rising in price and cited steel and aluminum among the main drivers of higher costs. That gap, with the index easing while purchasing managers report increases, usually means suppliers are still passing through increases set earlier.

## Where the negotiating leverage sits

Balanced. Easing prices give procurement teams a case to re-open contracts priced near the spring high. The duty and firm demand limit how far suppliers will move. Teams that can show the index decline and separate metal from conversion are in the strongest position.

## Supply risk: what could go wrong

- **Tariff changes** that move the regional premium quickly.
- **Energy costs** at smelters, which matter because aluminum production is very power-intensive.
- **Extrusion and casting capacity**, which can tighten even when metal is available.

## How to buy it now

1. **Re-quote anything priced at the spring peak.** Use the decline since June to ask for a reset, with the index data in hand.
2. **Split metal from conversion.** Price metal and premium off a published benchmark; negotiate the conversion charge on its own.
3. **Set the review period.** Monthly or quarterly metal adjustments keep price close to market.
4. **Compare domestic and imported landed cost.** Add the 50% duty to imported quotes.
5. **Keep a second source for critical extrusions.** Conversion capacity, not metal, is often the bottleneck.

## What to watch

- Whether the three-month decline continues in the next producer price releases.
- Changes to Section 232 coverage or country agreements.
- Regional premium moves, which show how much of the duty is reaching domestic prices.
